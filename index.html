Robotaxis: how safe is safe enough?

Today, the safety evidence for driverless vehicles comes mostly from the companies that operate them. This observatory shows what an independent, public register could look like in Switzerland: every type of incident, not only crashes, cross-checked against police, insurer and remote-operation data.

    Why the full picture matters

    Course lens (AI Safety and Human Factors, Days 1 and 2)

    • Autonomy is partly an illusion. Remote operators step in thousands of times a year. If those events are not reported, a fleet looks more autonomous than it is (Bradshaw et al., 2013, Myth 5).
    • Safety is more than the absence of crashes. Near-misses, violations and stranded vehicles show where the system is near its limits before anyone is hurt (Safety-II, Hollnagel).
    • Fleets fail together. One software behaviour is copied to every vehicle, so one weakness can strand hundreds of cars at once (see the San Francisco blackout case).
    • Observability builds trust. People can rely on a system when they can see its state and predict its behaviour (Johnson et al., 2018). A public register gives the whole of society that view.

    Evidence check: can we trust the numbers?

    Operators publish large crash statistics. This tool shows how much those numbers can actually prove, and what is missing when the company produces the evidence itself.

    How much does a crash count prove?

    Enter an observed number of events and the distance driven. The tool computes the rate with its confidence interval and compares it with a human benchmark.

    Expected rate for human drivers on the same roads.

    For scale: a RAND study (Kalra and Paddock, 2016) estimated that a fleet would need about 275 million failure-free miles (about 440 million km) to show with 95% confidence that its fatality rate is lower than that of US human drivers.

    Real · company data

    Waymo Safety Impact Hub

    95% fewer

    serious-injury-or-worse crashes than a human benchmark, over 271.3 million rider-only miles (to June 2026, five US cities). Also 82% fewer injury crashes and 82% fewer airbag-deployment crashes.

    Strengths

    • Method and crash data are published, with 95% confidence intervals by city.
    • Rates are compared per mile, not as raw counts.

    Limits for a regulator

    • The company runs the analysis and builds the human benchmark itself.
    • Miles driven are self-reported.
    • US city roads are not Swiss roads: trams, snow, mountain passes, different traffic rules.
    • Remote-assistance events are not part of the comparison.
    Real · insurer data, co-authored

    Swiss Re and Waymo claims study (Dec 2024)

    2 vs 26

    bodily-injury liability claims over 25.3 million driverless miles, against about 26 expected for human drivers over the same distance (92% fewer). Property damage: 9 claims vs 78 expected (88% fewer).

    Strengths

    • Uses a data source outside the company: insurance claims.
    • Large human baseline from the insurer's own portfolio.

    Limits for a regulator

    • Published jointly with Waymo, so not fully independent.
    • Both injury claims were still open when it was published.
    • Claims measure liability, not every safety-relevant event.

    Our proposal: from company claims to verified safety

    Switzerland is just starting driverless operations. Rules set now decide whether public trust rests on evidence.

    1 MARCH 2025

    The Ordinance on Automated Driving enters into force. Driverless vehicles may run only in operating areas approved by a canton, supervised by an operator.

    FEBRUARY 2026

    According to FEDRO, as reported by Vischer, no vehicle yet holds type approval for any of the three permitted uses. Type approval relies on a foreign authority plus a FEDRO conformity check.

    JUNE 2026

    FEDRO grants AmiGo (PostBus with Baidu's Apollo Go) a special permit for Level 4 trials in about 80 km² of St. Gallen and both Appenzells. Safety drivers stay on board. Driverless passenger service is planned for 2027.

    1 MARCH 2028

    All cantons must have set responsibilities and named their approval authorities for driverless operating areas.

    Status describes what we found in public summaries of the ordinance and FEDRO guidance; "proposed addition" means we ask for it to be specified, not that it is necessarily absent.

    New dangers the register must capture

    Deskilling

    Humans as backup monitors

    People asked to watch a system that is almost always right lose attention and skill. Remote supervisors watching many vehicles face the same trap.

    Case: Uber, Tempe 2018

    Fleet dynamics

    One flaw, hundreds of cars

    Identical software fails in the same way at the same time. Register metric: vehicles stranded at once, per event.

    Cases: Waymo SF blackout 2025, Cruise festival stall 2023

    Artificial crash causes

    Mistakes no human would make

    Behaviour like dragging a person while pulling over, or yielding to a school bus and then driving past it. These need their own categories.

    Cases: Cruise 2023, Waymo school buses 2025

    Teleoperation

    Driving through a screen

    Remote operators see through cameras, with delay and a narrow view. The register logs who was in control, latency and video quality.

    Case: Tesla Austin 2025–26

    A just culture for reporting

    Fines should target concealment and late reporting, as in the Cruise case, not honest reports. Remote supervisors and safety staff need a confidential channel and protection when they report problems. A punitive response to honest error reduces reporting, while a restorative approach asks who was hurt, what they need and what the system must change (Dekker and Breakey, 2016; Boskeljon-Horst et al., 2024). The register should publish learning, not blame.
